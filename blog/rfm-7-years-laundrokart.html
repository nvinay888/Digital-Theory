Seven years of RFM: what scaling Laundrokart's CRM taught us about retention

By Digitaltheory 8 Apr 2026 8 min read

When a business has seven years of transaction data and unit economics that are quietly degrading, the answer is rarely a new acquisition channel. It is almost always inside the data you already have. Here is what we found inside Laundrokart's.

Why RFM still wins

Retention frameworks come and go. Predictive ML, propensity scoring, real-time personalisation engines — all worth shipping, none of them mean anything if you can't first segment your customer base into cohorts you can run weekly. RFM (recency, frequency, monetary) is still the cleanest first cut.

The discipline it forces is useful: for each customer you compute when they last bought, how often they buy and how much they spend, score each dimension on a 1–5 scale, and bucket them into 12–15 cohort archetypes (champion, loyal, at-risk, hibernating, lost, new high-value, etc.). Then you decide what action belongs to each.

What seven years of laundry data said

We pulled Laundrokart's transaction history end-to-end — orders, channels, services, ratings, churn events. The story it told us was unforgiving:

  • The high-value cohort was tiny but compounding. The top 7% of customers were responsible for 38% of revenue — and crucially, they were still active, still buying, but receiving the same generic comms as the bottom 70%.
  • Win-back economics were inverted. The cost to reactivate a churned customer (via discount + push + WhatsApp) was actually lower than the cost to acquire a fresh install — we just weren't doing it.
  • Day-30 retention was the kill metric. Customers who came back inside 30 days of first order had a 4.6x higher 12-month LTV than those who came back at day 30–60. Anything after day 60 was effectively a lost customer, regardless of how much discount we threw at it.
  • Push fatigue was real. Notification open rates fell off a cliff past 4 sends per week. WhatsApp tolerated more because the format was different and the user opted in.

What we shipped

The CRM rebuild had four workstreams:

Cohort-based comms

Twelve cohorts, each with their own message frequency, channel mix and offer structure. Champions get refill nudges and early-access drops. At-risk customers get win-back triggers tied to the day-30 cliff. Hibernating customers get one big push and then stop hearing from us. Lost customers get a final discount and then suppression.

Day-30 trigger journeys

The single highest-impact change. A behavioural trigger fires if a first-time customer doesn't transact again within 25 days. The journey is push + WhatsApp + email + an in-app banner, sequenced over 5 days with declining intensity. Day-30 retention improved measurably and 12-month LTV moved with it.

Loyalty program for the top 7%

A tiered loyalty program designed explicitly for the high-value cohort that the data surfaced. Tiers came with bookable-priority service slots (rather than just discounts), which mattered more to a customer who was already paying full price. The program contributed about 3% of overall revenue inside a quarter — small in absolute terms but pure-margin and self-reinforcing.

Win-back coupons for lost customers

Targeted discounts to a specific cohort — lost-but-recoverable customers, defined by their RFM bucket — rather than blasted to the full file. Twelve percent additional revenue, and it didn't cannibalise active customers because they never saw the offer.

Numbers from the rebuild

Revenue up 45%. App installs up 55% (we paired CRM with rebuilt acquisition). LTV up 23%. Retention up 17 percentage points. The CRM work alone — net of acquisition changes — accounted for the majority of the LTV lift.

What we would tell you to do

If you have more than two years of transaction data, RFM gives you 80% of the ML value at 5% of the effort. Start there. The interesting bit isn't the segmentation — segmentation is easy. The interesting bit is what action you assign to each cohort, and the discipline of suppressing comms to cohorts where you don't have an action that pays back. Half of every retention program we touch is improved by sending less, not more.
